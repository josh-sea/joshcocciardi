      What this knows and what it's guessing

      Your distances. The starting numbers come from Shot Scope's tracked-shot data for male golfers at 5, 15 and 25 handicap, picked because it is measured rather than self-reported — shot-tracking studies find golfers overestimate their own carry by 10 to 20 percent. Your skill level is turned into a handicap, and the table is interpolated for it. Those are carry numbers. A distance the caddy measures from two GPS marks is total: carry plus roll. Total is the honest number for "can I reach that", so once a club has three logged shots its measured number takes over, and the card tells you which one it used.

      Elevation. Heights come from a terrain model, not your phone's altimeter, because GPS altitude is only good to tens of metres vertically. The terrain model does not know about a raised green. The adjustment uses TrackMan's one-to-one rule, a yard of distance per yard of climb; other rules in circulation are three times smaller, so treat it as a starting point.

      How far is left. Until you drop a flag, this is estimated: a typical hole length for the par you entered, scaled for the tees you are on, less the straight line from the tee to where your ball now sits. Straight line rather than the sum of your shots, because a sliced drive travels 200 yards without getting 200 yards closer. It is a decent guess on a normal hole and a poor one on a very long or very short hole, so drop a flag on the map when the number matters.

      Distance accuracy. A phone's GPS is typically good to 3 to 10 metres, which is 3 to 11 yards. The accuracy circle on the map is the real one your phone reports. That is fine for picking between a 7 and an 8 iron and not fine for arguing about a yardage.

      What it cannot see. Bunkers, water, trees, wind, lie and how the green sits. It measures ground you point at. It will happily send you straight over a lake, so look up.
